# The History of Gurzûl Khar

*This is original fictional worldbuilding created for this project. It is not a reproduction of any text by J.R.R. Tolkien or the Tolkien Estate, and claims no authenticity as a real Black Speech dialect — see DESIGN_DECISIONS.md §D4.*

## Origins

Within this project's invented lore, Gurzûl Khar ("the tongue of ash-iron," in later gloss) is told to have emerged among the garrison-clans stationed along the eastern approaches to Mordor — fortress communities too far from the Dark Tower for its speech to stay uniform, yet too central to the realm's war-economy to drift into a wholly separate language. Black Speech, as administered from the Tower, carried the weight of command and record; Gurzûl Khar is the form that command took after a few centuries of being shouted over furnace-noise, carved into gate-stones by masons who had never seen the capital, and passed down by officers who taught it to conscripts faster than they taught it correctly.

## Divergence from the parent tongue

Three forces are said to have shaped the dialect's sound and form:

1. **Furnace and forge.** The garrison-clans worked and lived beside smelting-works; their speech absorbed a bias toward the harsh fricatives and uvular sounds already present in Black Speech, sharpening them further. The consonant inventory's emphasis on velar, uvular and fricative sounds (documented in `data/phonology/phonology.json`) is explained in-world as a mark of this forge-adjacent life, not a simple inheritance.
2. **Administrative simplification.** Officers training large numbers of conscripts from many subject-peoples favoured a grammar that could be taught quickly. Gurzûl Khar's analytic verb system — no person/number agreement, tense carried by a single suffix (see DESIGN_DECISIONS.md §D2) — is explained as a direct consequence: a drill-sergeant's grammar, optimised for speed of teaching over expressive subtlety.
3. **Written record under pressure.** Where the Tower's scribes had leisure for an intricate hand, garrison clerks needed a script quick to carve into stone, wood, and iron under field conditions. This pressure is said to have produced the Khargûl script's severe, stem-and-stroke construction — a system any literate soldier could learn to both read and carve within a season, because every letter follows the same small set of rules rather than requiring memorisation of hundreds of unrelated shapes.

## The Khargûl script

Court record-keepers in the capital regarded the garrison hand as crude. Garrison scribes regarded the capital's hand as impractical for anything that had to survive weather, siege, or a mason's chisel. Over time, the garrison hand — the Khargûl script — became the de facto writing system of Gurzûl Khar specifically, distinct from (and, in the lore, never descended from) the writing conventions used elsewhere for Black Speech proper.

## Everyday and military use

Gurzûl Khar is imagined as having lived a double life: a military and administrative register used for orders, tallies, and fortification records, and a household register used by the garrison families who settled permanently near the eastern strongholds, raising children who spoke it as a first language rather than a drilled second one. This is why the lexicon spans both registers — words for war, rule, and dominion sit alongside words for family, food, weather, and the sky.

## Present day (in-world)

By the time of the events referenced in Tolkien's own works, Gurzûl Khar is imagined as a living regional dialect — not a dead administrative code — spoken by a shrinking population after the fall of its strongholds, its written tradition kept alive mostly by a handful of record-keepers and the stone inscriptions that happened to survive. This project's dictionary and grammar represent an attempt to reconstruct and continue that dialect as a usable constructed language in the present day, for learning and creative use — openly as a modern fan project, not a claim of real historical attestation.
